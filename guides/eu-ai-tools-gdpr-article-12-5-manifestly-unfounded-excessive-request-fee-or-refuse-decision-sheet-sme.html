Repeat, bulk or hostile data requests: a GDPR Article 12(5) decision sheet for when a small business may charge a fee or refuse, and the proof to keep

Updated 27 Oct 2026 · Disclosure: the EURO AI Register mentioned below is an Unadhi product with a free edition. This guide is for information only, not legal advice.

What you get: a finished Article 12(5) decision sheet in three tabs: a screening log with one row per request and a formula that sorts it into "answer free of charge", "combine with the open request", "possibly excessive" or "not a 12(5) case", a fee or refuse tab that records the planned-fee notice before you charge anything, and a proof file with the reply-by date and the three things every refusal letter must say, plus four made-up example rows.

Short answer: GDPR replies are free of charge. Only when a request is manifestly unfounded or excessive, "in particular because of their repetitive character", may you charge a reasonable fee based on administrative costs or refuse to act, and you carry the burden of showing it (Article 12(5) GDPR). The EDPB says these concepts must be interpreted narrowly, that a request is not excessive just because it gives no reason, uses rude language, takes a lot of effort or may lead to a claim, and that you should tell the person before charging a fee so they can withdraw (Guidelines 01/2022, paras. 175, 188, 189 and 194).

Why AI tools make this come up more

Small businesses now receive requests drafted with AI writing tools, sent through request services, or repeated in a chatbot several times a week. They can look like spam. Our reading of the EDPB guidance: the way a request is written is not a ground on its own, because there are "only very few prerequisites" for an access request (para. 177) and a request should not be regarded as excessive because no reasons are given or you regard it as meaningless (para. 189). What can matter is repetition with nothing changed, an overlap with a request you are still answering, or clear intent to disrupt. The 365-day line comes from the EDPB: a one-year interval between free answers "will in any case be too large" for the request to be considered excessive (example 41). It is not a minimum wait: a request a month later can be fine if the person has reason to think the data changed. "Don't know" in column I always gives "Answer free of charge". If the new request covers exactly the same information in scope and time, the EDPB treats it as a request for a further copy, for which Article 15(3) allows a reasonable fee (footnote 102).

Neither box is automatic: the EDPB says you are not free to pick either measure and must decide on the facts of each case (para. 192). Most small businesses will find that answering is quicker than defending a refusal.
