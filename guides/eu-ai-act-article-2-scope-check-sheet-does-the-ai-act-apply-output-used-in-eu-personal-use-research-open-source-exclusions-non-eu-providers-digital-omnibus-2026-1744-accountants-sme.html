Short answer: the AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) applies to providers that place AI systems on the EU market or put them into service in the EU wherever they are established, to deployers established or located in the EU, and to providers and deployers outside the EU where the output of the AI system is used in the EU; also to importers, distributors, product manufacturers and authorised representatives (Article 2(1)). It does not apply to exclusively military, defence or national security uses (2(3)), to AI developed and put into service for the sole purpose of scientific research (2(6)), to research, testing and development before a system is placed on the market or put into service, except testing in real-world conditions (2(8)), to deployers who are natural persons in a purely personal non-professional activity (2(10)), and to AI systems under free and open-source licences unless they are high-risk or fall under Article 5 or 50 (2(12)). Article 2 has applied since 2 February 2025 (Article 113(a)). Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 changed paragraphs 2 and 7 and added paragraph 13; the exclusions above were not changed.

Why clients ask their accountant this question

  • A non-EU client sells software into the EU, or an EU client buys work from an offshore team that uses AI, and wants to know whether the Act reaches it.
  • A small client uses everyday AI tools and assumes "we only use it, so it does not apply". Deployers established in the EU are in scope (Article 2(1)(b)); what changes is which duties follow.
  • A start-up is still testing or publishes its model as open source and wants to know when the rules start.
  • Bank, investor or customer questionnaires ask "is the AI Act applicable to you?" The sheet gives an answer with the paragraph behind it. Before you start, check the tool is an AI system at all with our "Is it an AI system?" check sheet.

Checklist: the exclusions and the test for each

  • Military, defence, national security (Art 2(3)): only where the system is placed on the market, put into service or used exclusively for those purposes. Dual use (for example also sold to civilian customers) is not excluded for the civilian use.
  • Third-country authorities (Art 2(4)): public authorities of a third country and international organisations using AI under law enforcement and judicial cooperation agreements with adequate safeguards. Rarely relevant to small business clients.
  • Sole-purpose scientific research (Art 2(6)): the system or model, including its output, must be specifically developed and put into service for the sole purpose of scientific research and development. Other AI systems used in research stay in scope (recital 25).
  • Before launch (Art 2(8)): research, testing and development before placing on the market or putting into service is outside the Act. Testing in real-world conditions is not excluded, and the Act applies as soon as the result is placed on the market or put into service (recital 25).
  • Purely personal use (Art 2(10)): covers only the obligations of deployers who are natural persons in a purely personal, non-professional activity. A sole trader using the same app for the business is a deployer (Art 3(4)).
  • Free and open-source licences (Art 2(12)): excluded unless placed on the market or put into service as a high-risk AI system or as a system under Article 5 or 50. Recital 103 says AI components that are provided against a price or otherwise monetised (for example through paid support) should not benefit from the open-source exceptions; publishing on an open repository is not in itself monetisation. Separate rules apply to open-source general-purpose AI models (Arts 53 and 54; recital 104).
  • Always still applies: EU data protection law (Art 2(7)); consumer protection and product safety law (Art 2(9)); more favourable national rules for workers (Art 2(11)).

Non-EU clients: what follows from "in scope"

  • A provider established outside the EU that makes a high-risk AI system available on the EU market must first appoint an authorised representative established in the EU by written mandate (Article 22(1)). See our Article 22 authorised representative guide.
  • Recital 22 explains the output rule: a third-country provider or deployer is covered to the extent the output is intended to be used in the EU, for example where an EU company contracts an offshore service that uses an AI system and sends the result back.
  • Whatever the role, the duties that already apply are AI literacy (Art 4) and the prohibited practices; check our Article 5 prohibited practices list and, for chatbots and generated content, the Article 50 disclosure checklist.

What the Digital Omnibus changed in Article 2

  • Paragraph 2 replaced: for high-risk systems under Article 6(1) linked to Annex I Section B products, only Article 6(1), Article 60a and Articles 102 to 112 apply; Articles 57 to 59 only as far as the requirements have been integrated in that legislation.
  • Paragraph 7 replaced: data protection law still applies; the cross-references now point to Articles 4a and 59 (Article 4a is the new rule on processing special categories of personal data for bias detection in high-risk systems).
  • Paragraph 13 added: for high-risk systems under Article 6(1), specific requirements in Articles 9 to 15 and 17 to 25 may be limited where Annex I Section A legislation gives equivalent or higher protection; the Commission is to adopt delegated acts by 2 August 2027.
  • Paragraphs 1, 3, 6, 8, 10 and 12 (the EU link and the exclusions used in the sheet) were not amended.

Limits and open points

Guidance not read: we built this guide from the text of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 as published (OJ L, 12.7.2024) and Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (OJ L, 24.7.2026) on EUR-Lex. We did not read Commission guidance or FAQs on scope for this guide, and no consolidated version; check them alongside this sheet. Open points: the Act does not define "output used in the Union" beyond recital 22, so offshore set-ups (row 11) need a case-by-case look. Whether a paid support contract around an open-source system removes the Article 2(12) exclusion is our reading of recital 103, which speaks of the open-source exceptions in general. National law: penalties and the competent authorities are set by each Member State (Article 99(1)); check the national rules where the client operates. The sheet sorts a list; it does not decide legal questions.

Frequently asked questions

Does the EU AI Act apply to a company outside the EU?

Yes, if it places AI systems on the EU market or puts them into service in the EU, or if the output of its AI system is used in the EU (Article 2(1)(a) and (c) of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). A non-EU provider of a high-risk system must also appoint an authorised representative in the EU (Article 22(1)).

Does the AI Act apply to a small business that only uses AI tools?

Yes. Deployers established or located in the EU are in scope (Article 2(1)(b)). Only natural persons using AI in a purely personal, non-professional activity are excluded (Article 2(10)). Which duties follow depends on the system, for example AI literacy, the prohibited practices and the Article 50 transparency duties.

Is open-source AI outside the AI Act?

AI systems released under free and open-source licences are excluded unless they are placed on the market or put into service as high-risk systems or as systems under Article 5 or 50 (Article 2(12)). Recital 103 says monetised components should not benefit from the open-source exceptions.

Does the AI Act apply while we are still testing?

No, research, testing and development before placing on the market or putting into service is excluded, but testing in real-world conditions is not (Article 2(8)). The Act applies once the system is placed on the market or put into service.
