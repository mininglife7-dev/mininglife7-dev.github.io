Short answer: a business that uses personal data (the controller) and its AI supplier (the processor) "shall cooperate, on request, with the supervisory authority" (GDPR Article 31). The authority may order them to provide any information it requires, notify an alleged infringement, and obtain access to personal data and to premises (Article 58(1)(a), (d), (e), (f)). The record of processing must be made available on request (Article 30(4)) and breaches must be documented so the authority can check them (Article 33(5)). The GDPR does not set a fixed reply period for these requests: the date is the one in the letter, under national procedure. Missing it, or refusing access, is itself a fineable failure (Article 83(5)(e)), while the degree of cooperation counts when fines are decided (Article 83(2)(f)).

Why clients bring these letters to their accountant

  • The accountant is often the first adviser who sees it, because it arrives with the post or in the general mailbox.
  • Much of what is asked sits with the accountant or the AI supplier: software lists, contracts, invoices showing which AI tools are paid for, staff numbers for the Article 30(5) question.
  • The deadline is short and set in the letter, often in days, and nobody owns it.
  • Accounting and tax firms are themselves bound by professional secrecy; GDPR Article 90 lets Member States adapt the authority's access powers for such firms, so access requests need a check first.

Step 2: checklist for reading the letter (10 minutes)

  • Who sent it: name of the authority, reference number, contact person. Check it is the real authority (address and phone on its own website, not in the email).
  • Is it a request, a notice or a decision? A request for information (Article 58(1)(a)), a notice of an alleged infringement (Article 58(1)(d)) or a binding measure under Article 58(2), such as an order to bring processing into compliance "within a specified period", a limitation or ban, or a fine. Recital 129 says a legally binding measure should be in writing, give reasons and refer to the right to an effective remedy.
  • The date: enter the reply-by date or the number of days into columns E or F. If there is no date, ask the authority which date applies.
  • Is there a complaint behind it? If a person complained (Article 77), look for the data request they sent first: an access, erasure or objection request that was missed is a common cause. The authority must keep the complainant informed of progress and outcome (Articles 57(1)(f) and 77(2)), so your reply will shape what the person is told.
  • Which AI tool and which supplier: the supplier must make available all information necessary to show it meets Article 28 (Article 28(3)(h)). Ask early.
  • Existing documents: record of processing (Article 30), breach documentation (Article 33(5)), DPIA (Article 35), processor agreement (Article 28), privacy notice (Articles 13 and 14). Send what exists; do not backdate what does not.
  • Professional secrecy: if access to an accounting or tax firm's client files is asked for, check the national rules adopted under Article 90 and the professional body's guidance first.

Limits and open points

Not read for this guide: we built it from the text of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR) on EUR-Lex. We did not read national procedural law on authority requests, deadlines and inspections, the national rules notified under Article 90, any EU regulation laying down additional procedural rules for cross-border GDPR enforcement, or EDPB and national authority guidance; check them for the client's country. Open points: the GDPR sets no fixed reply period for Article 58(1) requests; the period comes from the letter and national law. "Contact the authority before the date passes" and "do not backdate documents" are our practical advice, not rules in the text. The fine amounts in Article 83 are maximums; the amount depends on the Article 83(2) factors. The sheet sorts the work; it does not decide legal questions, and orders, fines and inspections call for a lawyer.

Frequently asked questions

Does a small business have to answer a letter from the data protection authority?

Yes. Controllers and processors shall cooperate, on request, with the supervisory authority (GDPR Article 31), and the authority may order them to provide any information it requires (Article 58(1)(a)). Failure to provide access in violation of Article 58(1) can be fined (Article 83(5)(e)).

How long does a business have to reply?

The GDPR does not set a fixed period for these requests. The reply date is the one in the authority's letter, under national procedural law. If no date is given, ask the authority.

Must the record of processing be sent?

The record of processing activities must be made available to the supervisory authority on request (GDPR Article 30(4)). Enterprises with fewer than 250 employees are exempt from keeping it only in the cases set out in Article 30(5).

Can the AI supplier be asked for documents?

Yes. The processor must make available to the controller all information necessary to demonstrate compliance with GDPR Article 28 and allow for and contribute to audits (Article 28(3)(h)). The processor must also cooperate with the authority on request (Article 31).
