Using AI lead-enrichment tools or bought contact lists? A GDPR Article 14 notice sheet for small businesses: what to tell people and by when

Updated 24 Oct 2026 · Disclosure: the EURO AI Register mentioned below is an Unadhi product with a free edition. This guide is for information only, not legal advice.

What you get: a finished Article 14 notice sheet: one row per contact source you did not collect from the person yourself (AI lead-enrichment tools, bought or rented lists, trade fair badge scans, lists from partners), a deadline formula for when the notice must reach people, one column per information check from Article 14(1) and 14(2), the objection line Article 21 asks for, a result formula, three made-up example rows and first-message wording you can adapt.

Short answer: when you get someone's personal data from somewhere other than that person, Article 14 GDPR says you must tell them who you are, why you use their data, where it came from and what their rights are. The deadline is the earliest of: one month after you got the data, your first message to them, or the first time you pass the data on. Data from public websites is not exempt: the notice must say whether the source was public.

Step 1: which of your contact data is an Article 14 case?

  • Article 13 applies when the person gives you the data (a web form, a call, a booking). Use the Article 13 privacy notice AI section sheet for those.
  • Article 14 applies to everything else: emails and job titles added by an AI lead-enrichment tool, bought or rented lists, contacts collected from websites by a crawler, trade fair badge scans from the organiser, names passed on by a partner.
  • Fields the AI tool guesses (seniority, buying intent, company size) are still personal data about the person when they are linked to a name. List them in column B.

First-message wording (adapt to your business)

"We are [business name, address, contact email]. We found your work contact details through [source, e.g. an AI lead-enrichment tool / a list from (supplier) / your company's public website] on [month]. We use your name, job title and work email to [purpose] on the basis of our legitimate interest in [interest]. We keep them for [period] unless you ask us to stop. [Recipients and any transfer outside the EU with the safeguard.] You can ask for access, correction, erasure, restriction or portability, and you can complain to [supervisory authority]. Our full notice: [link]."

Separate line, clearly visible: "You can object to us using your details at any time, including for marketing: reply 'stop' and we will no longer use them for that purpose."

Email and phone marketing also fall under your country's rules implementing the ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC. Those rules decide whether you may send the first message at all; Article 14 only decides what it must say.

If you want to claim an exception

  • Already has the information (Art. 14(5)(a)): write down how they got it and when. A partner's own notice only counts if it covered your business and your purpose.
  • Disproportionate effort (Art. 14(5)(b)): Recital 62 points to the number of people, the age of the data and the safeguards. Write down your reasons and take appropriate measures, including making the information publicly available. If you are going to email the person anyway, Article 14(3)(b) already gives you a moment to tell them, so this exception is usually hard to argue.
  • Laid down by law or professional secrecy (Art. 14(5)(c) and (d)): note the exact law. Accountants should check their own secrecy rules before relying on this.
