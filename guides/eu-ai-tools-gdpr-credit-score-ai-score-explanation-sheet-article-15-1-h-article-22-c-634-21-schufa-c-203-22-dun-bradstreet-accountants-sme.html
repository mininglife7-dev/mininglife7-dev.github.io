Short answer: since C-634/21 SCHUFA (7 December 2023), a credit agency's automatically calculated probability score is itself an "automated individual decision" under GDPR Article 22(1) where a third party "draws strongly" on it to establish, implement or terminate a contract with the person. Since C-203/22 Dun & Bradstreet Austria (27 February 2025), the person can require the controller to explain "the procedure and principles actually applied" to their data, "in a concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible form" (Article 15(1)(h)). Sending a complex mathematical formula or an algorithm, or describing every step, is not enough (para 59). Trade secrets are not a reason to refuse outright: the controller has to give the withheld information to the data protection authority or court, which balances the interests (ruling 2).

Why this lands on the accountant's desk

  • Clients use scores without calling them decisions: pay-later and invoice checks at an online shop's checkout, tenant screening, credit limits, AI CV screeners, fraud tools that block accounts. If the outcome nearly always follows the score, the client may be running Article 22 decisions.
  • The request arrives as an access request: "why was I refused?" is an Article 15 request with a one-month clock (Article 12(3)). Accountants and bookkeepers are often the ones asked to find the data and the supplier's terms.
  • Supplier questionnaires and audits now ask whether the business uses automated decisions, which exception in Article 22(2) applies and how a person can contest.

Step 2: the 10-question checklist (one score at a time)

  1. Is there a decision? The Court reads "decision" broadly; it can include the result of calculating a person's creditworthiness as a probability value (C-634/21 para 46). Recital 71 names "automatic refusal of an online credit application or e-recruiting practices without any human intervention".
  2. Is it based solely on automated processing? Ask how often staff actually change the outcome. If the answer is "almost never", record why the review is still real or treat it as automated (our reading).
  3. Does it have legal or similarly significant effects? Refusal of a contract, worse terms, a job rejection, a blocked account. A marketing segment usually does not (our reading).
  4. Is the score from a supplier? If you draw strongly on it, the supplier's score is itself an Article 22 decision (C-634/21, ruling). Ask the supplier how it handles Article 15(1)(h) requests.
  5. Which Article 22(2) exception applies? Necessary for a contract, authorised by Union or Member State law with safeguards, or explicit consent. If none, stop the solely automated decision.
  6. Is health or other Article 9(1) data used? Then only explicit consent or substantial public interest law, with safeguards (Article 22(4)).
  7. Can the person get human intervention, express their view and contest? Required at least for the contract and consent cases (Article 22(3)).
  8. Does the privacy notice say so? Articles 13(2)(f) and 14(2)(g) require the existence of the automated decision, meaningful information about the logic, and the significance and envisaged consequences.
  9. Is a plain explanation ready? Which data, how they counted, and, per C-203/22 para 62, possibly the extent to which a change in the data would have led to a different result.
  10. Is a request open? Reply within one month of receipt; extension by two further months only where necessary, with reasons given within the first month (Article 12(3)). Include a copy of the data, including the score (Article 15(1) and (3)).

Frequently asked questions

Is a credit agency score an automated decision even if we make the final call?

The Court of Justice held that the automated establishment of a probability value by a credit information agency is an automated individual decision under Article 22(1) where the third party it is sent to draws strongly on it to establish, implement or terminate a contract (C-634/21, 7 December 2023). Whether you draw strongly on it depends on how often your outcome simply follows the score.

Do we have to give out the scoring algorithm?

No. The Court said the mere communication of a complex mathematical formula, such as an algorithm, or a detailed description of all the steps is not a sufficiently concise and intelligible explanation (C-203/22, para 59). The person must be able to understand which of their data were used and in what way (para 61).

Can we refuse because the scoring method is a trade secret?

Not on your own. Where the controller considers the information contains trade secrets or other people's data, it must provide that information to the competent supervisory authority or court, which balances the rights and interests to decide how much the person gets (C-203/22, ruling 2).

How long do we have to answer?

Without undue delay and in any event within one month of receipt of the request. The period may be extended by two further months where necessary, taking into account complexity and number of requests, and the person must be told of the extension and the reasons within the first month (GDPR Article 12(3)).
