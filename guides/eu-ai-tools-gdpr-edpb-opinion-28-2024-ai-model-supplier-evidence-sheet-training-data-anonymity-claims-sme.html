Was the AI model you use trained lawfully? A supplier evidence sheet from EDPB Opinion 28/2024 (training data sources, anonymity claims, legitimate interest) for small businesses and their accountants

Updated 28 Oct 2026 · Disclosure: the EURO AI Register mentioned below is an Unadhi product with a free edition. This guide is for information only, not legal advice.

What you get: a finished AI model supplier evidence sheet in three tabs: a model list with one row per AI tool you feed personal data into and a result formula that sorts each row into STOP, ASK, RECORD or LOW; a supplier question template you can paste into an email, built from the documentation list in EDPB Opinion 28/2024; and a legitimate interest note for the cases where you rely on Article 6(1)(f) GDPR. Four made-up example rows show how it fills in.

Short answer: if you deploy someone else's AI model and process personal data with it, the EDPB says supervisory authorities "should take into account whether the controller deploying the model conducted an appropriate assessment" to ascertain that the model "was not developed by unlawfully processing personal data", looking at criteria such as "the source of the data" and whether a supervisory authority or a court has found an infringement (Opinion 28/2024, para. 129). A supplier simply saying the model is anonymous is not enough: "a mere assertion of anonymity of the model is not enough to exempt it from the application of the GDPR" (para. 134). How deep you go depends on the risk (para. 130). This sheet records that assessment.

Why this matters for a small business

Under Article 5(2) GDPR the controller "shall be responsible for, and be able to demonstrate compliance". The EDPB applies this to AI models: "each controller should ensure the lawfulness of the processing it conducts and be able to demonstrate it", so authorities may look both at the developer and at "the controller that acquired the AI model and processes the personal data by itself" (para. 126). Where processing was unlawful, an authority may order corrective measures that can go as far as, depending on the facts and proportionality, "the erasure of the whole dataset used to develop the AI model and/or the AI model itself" (para. 114). If your workflows depend on one model, you want to know before that happens.

Two limits to keep in mind. The Opinion is addressed to supervisory authorities, and its observations "should be assessed on a case-by-case basis" (para. 112). And it deals with lawfulness (Article 5(1)(a) and Article 6 GDPR); other issues such as transparency or data protection by design are "not analysed in this Opinion" (para. 111).

A high-risk AI system comes with an EU declaration of conformity, which must contain, where personal data is processed, "a statement that that AI system complies with Regulations (EU) 2016/679" (AI Act Annex V, point 5; Article 47). File it, but do not stop there: the EDPB says "such a self-declaration may not constitute a conclusive finding of compliance under the GDPR" (para. 131).

Step 2: tab 2, supplier question template

Paste these into one email to the vendor. Questions 4 to 7 follow the documentation the EDPB says authorities should ideally see when a model is claimed to be anonymous (para. 58).

#Question to the supplierSource
1Which AI model or models does your service use, and who developed each one?Para. 125 (roles of each actor)
2Describe the sources of the training data, including any public web data and any third-party datasets. If the model is a general-purpose AI model, please send the link to its public training content summary.Para. 129; para. 58(c); AI Act Art. 53(1)(d)
3Has any data protection authority or court found that personal data was processed unlawfully to develop the model? Did any training data come from a personal data breach?Paras. 129, 130
4If you say the model is anonymous: please share the DPIA information (or the decision that none was needed) and any advice from your DPO.Para. 58(a), (b)
5What technical and organisational measures did you take to reduce the likelihood of identification, for each source of training data?Para. 58(c), (d)
6How was the model tested against attacks such as regurgitation of training data, membership inference and model inversion: by whom, when, how and with what results?Para. 55; para. 58(e)
7What documentation do you give deployers about these measures and about residual risks?Para. 58(f)
8If personal data in the model is erased or restricted, will you tell us?Para. 127; Art. 17(1)(d), Art. 19 GDPR

Keep the reply, or a note that no reply came, as the evidence for the row. The EDPB adds that passing tests against known attacks "can only be evidence for the resistance to those attacks" (para. 55).

Step 3: tab 3, legitimate interest note

If your own use of the model relies on legitimate interest (Article 6(1)(f) GDPR), the EDPB says the unlawfulness of the initial processing "should be taken into account as part of the legitimate interest assessment" (para. 132; see also para. 123). Add three lines to your assessment for each model:

LineWhat to recordSource
Technical limitsFilters or access limits built into the model that you cannot switch off and that may stop personal data being disclosedPara. 132
Legal factsWhat is known about the nature and severity of any unlawful processing in development (from column G)Para. 132
Expectations and risksWhether the people affected would expect your use, and the risks to the people whose data was used for trainingParas. 123, 132

If a supplier shows that the model was anonymised and your use of it does not process personal data from training, the EDPB considers the GDPR would not apply to that operation, and the lawfulness of the personal data you then collect in deployment "should not be impacted by the unlawfulness of the initial processing" (paras. 134, 135). Your own processing still has to meet the GDPR on its own terms.
