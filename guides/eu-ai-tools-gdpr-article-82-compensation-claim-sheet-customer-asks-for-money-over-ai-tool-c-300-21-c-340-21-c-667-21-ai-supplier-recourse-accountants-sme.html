Short answer: "Any person who has suffered material or non-material damage as a result of an infringement" of the GDPR can claim compensation from the controller or processor (GDPR Article 82(1)). The Court of Justice has said that an infringement alone is not enough: damage and a causal link must exist, but national courts may not demand that non-material damage reach a minimum level of seriousness (C-300/21, 4 May 2023). Fear of misuse of leaked data can itself be non-material damage, and after a hack the business must prove its security measures were appropriate (C-340/21, 14 December 2023). Compensation makes good the damage actually suffered and is not a punishment (C-667/21, 21 December 2023). When the AI supplier is part of the same processing, each one responsible can be held liable for the entire damage and can then claim back the other's share (Article 82(4)-(5)).

Why clients bring these claims to their accountant

  • It is a money question first: should a provision be booked, does the insurance cover it, can the AI supplier be asked to pay part?
  • Claims letters now often follow a data request or a complaint, and the accountant has the supplier contracts and invoices that show which AI tool was used.
  • Payroll bureaus and bookkeepers are often the processor themselves: Article 82(2) makes a processor liable only in two cases, and the sheet sorts those.
  • Small amounts add up: a voucher for one customer can become a pattern if the cause (a chatbot setting, a missing notice) is not fixed.

Step 2: checklist for the first read of the claim (10 minutes)

  • Who claims, for what, and how much: name, the event, the amount and any date to reply. Log the date the letter arrived.
  • What infringement is alleged? Article 82(1) needs an infringement of the GDPR, damage and a link between the two. The Court of Justice held that "the mere infringement" of the GDPR "is not sufficient to confer a right to compensation" (C-300/21, operative part 1).
  • What damage is described? Material (money lost, costs) or non-material (distress, loss of control, fear of misuse). No minimum seriousness may be required for non-material damage (C-300/21, operative part 2), and fear of misuse "is capable, in itself" of being non-material damage (C-340/21, operative part 6).
  • Was it a breach or hack? An unauthorised disclosure by a third party does not by itself mean the security measures were inadequate, but in a damages action the controller bears the burden of proving they were appropriate (C-340/21, operative parts 1, 3 and 5). Collect the security record now: access settings, two-factor login, retention settings, the supplier's security terms.
  • Who else was involved? Find the AI supplier contract. A processor is liable only where it breached obligations aimed at processors or acted outside or contrary to the controller's lawful instructions (Article 82(2)); a processor that decides purposes and means is treated as a controller for that processing (Article 28(10)).
  • The exemption is narrow: a controller or processor is exempt only "if it proves that it is not in any way responsible for the event giving rise to the damage" (Article 82(3)). Liability rests on a fault that is presumed unless the business proves the event is in no way attributable to it (C-667/21, operative part 5).
  • Insurance and provision: check the policy's notice clause; discuss with the client whether a provision or a note is needed under the accounting rules that apply to them.
  • Is a court case already filed? Proceedings may be brought in the Member State where the controller or processor has an establishment or where the person habitually resides (Articles 79(2) and 82(6)). A not-for-profit body may claim compensation on the person's behalf where national law allows (Article 80(1)).

Limits and open points

Not read for this guide: we built it from the text of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR) and the operative parts of judgments C-300/21, C-340/21 and C-667/21, all on EUR-Lex. We did not read later Court of Justice judgments on Article 82, national rules on limitation periods, procedure, costs and the amount of damages, national rules on representative actions (Article 80 and Directive (EU) 2020/1828), insurance terms, accounting standards on provisions, or EDPB and national guidance; check them for the client's country. Open points: the GDPR sets no reply period for a compensation claim: the 14-day internal default and "do not admit liability before advice" are our practical advice, not rules in the text. Whether a claim succeeds is for the national court; the sheet sorts the work and does not decide legal questions, and court claims call for a lawyer.

Frequently asked questions

Can a customer get compensation just because a GDPR rule was broken?

No. The Court of Justice held that the mere infringement of the GDPR is not sufficient to confer a right to compensation under Article 82(1); damage must have been suffered as a result (C-300/21, 4 May 2023). But national courts may not require non-material damage to reach a minimum level of seriousness.

Is fear that leaked data will be misused enough?

It can be. The Court of Justice held that the fear experienced by a person about possible misuse of their data by third parties after a GDPR infringement is capable, in itself, of constituting non-material damage (C-340/21, 14 December 2023).

Who must prove that security was adequate after a hack?

The controller. In an action for damages under Article 82, the controller bears the burden of proving that its security measures were appropriate under Article 32, and a hack by a third party does not on its own exempt it (C-340/21).

Can the AI supplier be made to pay part?

Where the controller and the AI supplier are both involved in the same processing and both responsible, each can be held liable for the entire damage, and the one who paid can claim back the other's share (GDPR Article 82(4)-(5)). A processor is liable only for breaching processor duties or acting outside lawful instructions (Article 82(2)).
