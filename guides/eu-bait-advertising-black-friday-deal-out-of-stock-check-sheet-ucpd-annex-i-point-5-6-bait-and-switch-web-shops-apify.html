Short answer: a deal that sells out is not illegal. What is banned, in all circumstances, is advertising a product “at a specified price without disclosing the existence of any reasonable grounds the trader may have for believing that he will not be able to offer for supply … those products or equivalent products at that price for a period that is, and in quantities that are, reasonable having regard to the product, the scale of advertising of the product and the price offered” (UCPD Annex I point 5, “bait advertising”). If stock is short, say so in the ad. Point 6 (“bait and switch”) bans advertising at a price and then refusing to show, take orders for or deliver the item within a reasonable time, or showing a defective sample, “with the intention of promoting a different product”.

Why accountants, bookkeepers and web agencies should look at this before Black Friday

  • Black Friday is 27 November 2026 (18 days from 9 November). Newsletters, homepage banners and paid ads with a deal price go out in the next two weeks; each one with a product and a price is an invitation to purchase (Article 2(i)).
  • No case-by-case test. Annex I practices are unfair “in all circumstances” (Article 5(5)). The bigger the campaign, the more stock point 5 expects: it measures quantity against “the scale of advertising”.
  • Proof is on the shop. Under Article 12 an authority or court can ask the trader to prove a factual claim and treat it as inaccurate if the proof is missing. Column N makes sure proof exists.

Launch-morning checklist (per advertised deal)

  1. Is the shop selling to consumers? B2B-only shops are outside the UCPD (Article 3(1)) — column C.
  2. Does every ad with a product and a deal price lead to a page with that price or lower? (Annex I point 5 “at that price”; Article 6(1)(d)) — columns E–F.
  3. Is the product in stock at launch? An advertised deal that is sold out the moment it goes live is the classic bait ad — column G.
  4. Does stock at the deal price cover the orders the client expects, given how widely the deal is advertised? If not, does the ad say “only [n] units at this price”? — columns H–J.
  5. If the deal sells out within hours and no limit was stated, look again at how much stock there was (column K).
  6. Is the sold-out page pushing a different, dearer product while orders for the advertised one are refused or not delivered? That is the point 6 pattern — columns L–M.
  7. Keep the dated ad screenshot, the stock export at launch and the scraper price snapshot (Article 12) — column N.

Safe ad wording

Black Friday: [product] for EUR [price] (was EUR [30-day lowest price]).
Only [n] units at this price, from [day date, time, time zone]
until [day date, time, time zone] or until sold out.

Avoid: advertising a deal price for a product you will not have in stock at launch; leaving a sold-out deal in paid ads and newsletters; sending buyers of the advertised item to a different, dearer product while their orders are refused.

Frequently asked questions

Is it illegal to advertise a Black Friday deal that sells out?

Not as such. Selling out is fine. UCPD Annex I point 5 bans, in all circumstances, making an invitation to purchase products at a specified price without disclosing any reasonable grounds the trader may have for believing that it will not be able to supply those products, or equivalent products, at that price for a period and in quantities that are reasonable having regard to the product, the scale of advertising and the price offered. If the shop expects to run short, it must say so in the ad.

How do we disclose a limited quantity?

Say it in the ad itself, for example 'only 50 units at this price' or 'deal price while stock lasts, 50 units'. Point 5 is about not disclosing; a clear limit in the invitation to purchase removes the point 5 problem. The limit must itself be true: a false 'only 50 left' claim is a different problem under Annex I point 7 and Article 6(1)(b).

What is bait and switch?

UCPD Annex I point 6: making an invitation to purchase at a specified price and then refusing to show the advertised item, refusing to take orders for it or deliver it within a reasonable time, or demonstrating a defective sample of it, with the intention of promoting a different product. The sheet flags rows where another product is pushed while the advertised one is refused or not available.

Does a newsletter or Instagram post count as an invitation to purchase?

It can. Article 2(i) UCPD defines an invitation to purchase as a commercial communication which indicates characteristics of the product and the price in a way appropriate to the means used and thereby enables the consumer to make a purchase. The Commission Guidance (2021/C 526/01, section 2.9.5) says it does not need to include an actual way to buy, and that a radio advert with a product's characteristics and price is an invitation to purchase.

What if the product page shows a higher price than the advert?

Then the product is not offered 'at that price', which is the point 5 test, and false information on the price is a misleading action under Article 6(1)(d). The Commission Guidance (section 2.9.5) says advertising prices that do not exist could also breach points 5 and 6 of Annex I.

Why keep proof?

Article 12 UCPD lets courts and authorities require the trader to furnish evidence as to the accuracy of factual claims and to consider the claims inaccurate if the evidence is missing or insufficient. A dated screenshot of the ad, a stock export at launch and a price snapshot from the product scraper are simple proof.
