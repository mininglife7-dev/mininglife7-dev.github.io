Emotion AI at work and with customers: an EU AI Act check sheet (Article 5(1)(f) ban, Article 50(3) notice) for call centres, shops and hiring

Updated 27 Oct 2026 · Disclosure: the EURO AI Register mentioned below is an Unadhi product with a free edition. This guide is for information only, not legal advice.

What you get: a finished emotion AI check sheet in three tabs: a feature list where you enter every AI feature that scores mood, tone, stress, "sentiment" or engagement (call analytics, interview tools, meeting assistants, in-store cameras, learning apps), with a result formula that sorts each one into STOP, REVIEW, ALLOWED WITH DUTIES or OUT OF SCOPE; a notice record for the Article 50(3) duty to tell people; and a dates and actions tab, plus five made-up example rows.

Short answer: two questions decide almost everything. Whose emotions? Using AI "to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions" has been banned since 2 February 2025, except for medical or safety reasons (Article 5(1)(f)). From what input? The emotion recognition rules only cover inferences "on the basis of their biometric data" such as voice, face or typing rhythm (Article 3(39)); the Commission says inferring emotions from written text is out of scope (Guidelines C(2025) 5052, paras. 251, 265). Emotion recognition on customers is not banned by Article 5(1)(f), but since 2 August 2026 deployers must "inform the natural persons exposed thereto" (Article 50(3)), and it is listed as high-risk in Annex III, point 1(c).

Why this matters now

Many customer service, sales call and video meeting tools now ship a "sentiment", "mood" or "engagement" score switched on by default. If the score is about your own staff, job candidates or students, and it is built from their voice, face or keystrokes, it can fall under the ban, with fines for breaching Article 5 of up to EUR 35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, and for SMEs the lower of the two (Article 99(3) and (6)). The Commission's own examples include "Using webcams and voice recognition systems by a call centre to track their employee's emotions, such as anger, is prohibited" and "Using emotion recognition AI systems during the recruitment process is prohibited" (Guidelines, para. 254).

Read the narrow exceptions carefully. The Commission says the medical or safety exception "should be narrowly interpreted", that "general monitoring of stress levels at the workplace is not permitted", that a system to detect burnout or depression at work "would remain prohibited", and that safety means protecting life and health, not property "against theft or fraud" (Guidelines paras. 256 to 258). The training-only route is allowed only "if the results are not shared with HR responsible persons and cannot impact the assessment, promotion etc." of the person trained (para. 254).

Customer-facing emotion AI can still be banned in other ways: the Commission notes such systems "may be prohibited in certain cases by virtue of Article 5(1)(a) and (b)" (harmful manipulation and exploitation), and that data protection and consumer protection law continue to apply (Guidelines para. 266). Member States and collective agreements may also set rules more favourable to workers on AI at work (Art. 2(11), as quoted in Guidelines para. 264).
