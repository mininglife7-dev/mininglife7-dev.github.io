Short answer: in the EU, marketing by email "may be allowed only in respect of subscribers or users who have given their prior consent" (ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC, Article 13(1)). The one exception, often called the soft opt-in, is narrow: you obtained the customer's email address "in the context of the sale of a product or a service", you are "the same natural or legal person", you market "its own similar products or services", and the customer was given the chance to object "free of charge and in an easy manner" when the address was collected and "on the occasion of each message" (Article 13(2)). Using an AI tool to write the email changes none of this.

Why this matters before Black Friday

Many small shops, studios and service firms will send their biggest campaign of the year around Black Friday (Friday 27 November 2026), often to a list exported from the shop or booking system and with text drafted by an AI writing tool. The ePrivacy Directive treats email as cheap to send but a burden on the recipient, which is why it requires prior consent as the rule (Recital 40). The existing-customer exception exists because "within the context of an existing customer relationship, it is reasonable to allow the use of electronic contact details for the offering of similar products or services, but only by the same company that has obtained the electronic contact details" (Recital 41).

The Court of Justice confirmed in Inteligo Media (Case C-654/23, judgment of 13 November 2025) that Article 13(2) is an exception to the consent rule that "must, therefore, be interpreted strictly" (para. 59), and set out its conditions in order: address obtained in the context of a sale, marketing of similar products or services "provided by that sender itself", and a free, easy objection at collection and in each message (para. 48). The same judgment held that a free account which gives access to some content and leads towards paid content can count as obtaining the address "in the context of the sale of a product or a service" (paras. 54 to 57 and 63), and that where an email is sent in line with Article 13(2), the lawful-processing conditions of Article 6(1) GDPR "are not applicable" to that use of the address (para. 69, reading Article 95 GDPR).

One limit: Article 13 is in a directive, so each Member State applies it through its own law. Article 13(3) leaves some choices to national legislation, and Article 13(5) says paragraphs 1 and 3 apply to subscribers who are natural persons, with Member States protecting the legitimate interests of other subscribers such as companies. Check your country's rule for business addresses.

Two AI-specific points. If an AI tool segments the list or picks offers per customer, that is profiling related to direct marketing, and an objection covers it too: the right to object "includes profiling to the extent that it is related to such direct marketing" (Art. 21(2) GDPR). And pasting the customer list into an AI tool is a separate use of personal data from sending the email; check it with the related sheets linked below. Having a person read every AI-drafted email against checks 1 to 6 before sending is our working rule.
