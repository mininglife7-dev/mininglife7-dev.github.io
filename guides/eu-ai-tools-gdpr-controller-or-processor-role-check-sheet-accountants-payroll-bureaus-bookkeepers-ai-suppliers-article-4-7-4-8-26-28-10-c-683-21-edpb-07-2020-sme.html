Short answer: the controller is whoever "alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing"; a processor "processes personal data on behalf of the controller" (GDPR Article 4(7) and 4(8)). The EDPB uses the accountant as its own example: an accounting firm that audits a client under the rules of its profession, without detailed instructions, decides the purpose and means itself and is a controller; where no law sets the firm's duties and the client gives very detailed instructions, the firm "would indeed be acting as a processor" (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, version 2.1, para 40). A payroll bureau following the employer's clear instructions on who to pay, what, when and how long to keep the data is the EDPB's example of a processor. And an AI supplier that starts using client data for its own purposes, such as its own business, is treated as a controller for that processing and infringes the GDPR (Article 28(10), EDPB para 81).

Why this question lands on the accountant's desk

  • Clients and their customers send questionnaires asking "are you our processor?" and expect a signed Article 28 contract. If the firm is in fact a controller for the audit or tax work, signing one for that work misstates the role; if it is a processor for payroll, the contract is required.
  • The role decides the duties: a controller needs a legal basis, a privacy notice (Articles 13 and 14) and controller records (Article 30(1)); a processor needs a contract with the Article 28(3) points, written instructions and processor records (Article 30(2)).
  • AI suppliers change the picture: an AI invoice reader used for client payroll is a sub-processor that needs the client's authorisation (Article 28(2)); an AI chatbot whose terms allow training on chats may be using the data for its own purposes (Article 28(10)).
  • Joint control happens without a contract: the Court of Justice held that two entities can be joint controllers without any arrangement between them (C-683/21, operative part 2), so a joint webinar or shared lead list with a law firm needs checking.

Step 2: the 9-question role checklist (one service at a time)

  1. Why is the data processed, and for whom? Deciding the purpose is always for the controller; a processor "can never determine the purpose" (EDPB para 36).
  2. Who decides the essential means? Which data, for how long, who gets access and whose data: these are reserved to the controller. Software choice and detailed security can be left to a processor (EDPB para 40).
  3. Do professional rules set the task? For an audit done under the rules of the profession without detailed client instructions, the EDPB treats the accounting firm as a controller; it says the answer may differ where the processing is a limited, ancillary task (EDPB para 40, accountants example).
  4. Are there written instructions? A processor processes "only on documented instructions from the controller" (Article 28(3)(a)), and anyone acting under the controller's or processor's authority processes only on the controller's instructions (Article 29).
  5. Does anyone use the data for a purpose of its own? If a processor determines purposes and means, it "shall be considered to be a controller in respect of that processing" (Article 28(10)). Read the AI supplier's terms on model training and product improvement.
  6. Is another firm deciding with you? Joint control can come from a common decision or from converging decisions where the processing would not be possible without both (EDPB paras 54-55). No arrangement is needed for joint control to exist (C-683/21, operative part 2), but joint controllers must then set out their responsibilities in an arrangement, make its essence available, and people can use their rights against each of them (Article 26(1)-(3)).
  7. Is it only one stage? A website operator embedding a third-party plugin was a controller only for the collection and transmission it decided, not for what the plugin provider did afterwards (C-40/17 Fashion ID, operative part 2, decided under the old Directive 95/46). Under the same directive, the administrator of a fan page on a social network was held to be a controller (C-210/16 Wirtschaftsakademie, operative part 1).
  8. Did you commission a tool without touching the data? An entity that had an app developed and took part in deciding its purposes and means can be a controller even without processing the data itself, unless it expressly objected before release (C-683/21, operative part 1).
  9. Is it really personal data? Using personal data to test software is processing unless the data are anonymised so the person is no longer identifiable, or are fictitious (C-683/21, operative part 3).

Frequently asked questions

Is an accountant a controller or a processor under the GDPR?

It depends on the service. In the EDPB example, an accounting firm that audits a client under the rules of its profession, without detailed instructions, is a controller; where no law sets the firm's duties and the client gives very detailed instructions, it acts as a processor (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para 40). The same firm can be a controller for one service and a processor for another (para 82).

Is a payroll bureau a processor?

In the EDPB example, yes: a payroll administrator that pays salaries on the employer's clear instructions on who to pay, what amounts, by what date, how long to keep the data and what to send to the tax authority is a processor, even if it chooses its own software, as long as it does not go against or beyond those instructions (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para 40).

What if our AI supplier trains its model on our clients' data?

If a processor infringes the GDPR by determining the purposes and means of processing, it is considered a controller in respect of that processing (Article 28(10)); the EDPB says adding a purpose of its own makes the provider a controller for that processing and is an infringement (para 81). Ask the supplier in writing and switch the setting off or change the terms.

Do we need a contract to be joint controllers?

No. The Court of Justice held that classifying two entities as joint controllers does not require an arrangement between them (C-683/21, 5 December 2023, operative part 2). But once they are joint controllers, Article 26 requires them to set out their responsibilities in an arrangement and make its essence available to the people concerned.
