What you get: a finished sub-processor change sheet for accountants, tax advisers and bookkeepers whose small business clients receive emails such as "we are updating our list of sub-processors" from their AI tools (chatbots, note-takers, invoice readers, email assistants). One row per notice: type in eleven short answers and the sheet returns the object-by date, the days left, a result (ACCEPT, ASK SUPPLIER, OBJECT, DECIDE, STOP or NOTE ONLY) and a to-do list with the GDPR Article behind each step. Also included: a checklist for reading a notice, a copy-ready question email to the supplier, a copy-ready objection email and a client email. Formulas tested in LibreOffice on nine made-up rows.

Short answer: under GDPR Article 28(2) an AI supplier that processes personal data for your client (a processor) may not bring in another processor without the client's prior specific or general written authorisation. With a general authorisation the supplier must tell the client about any intended addition or replacement, "thereby giving the controller the opportunity to object". The GDPR does not set the notice period; the contract does. The Commission's standard contractual clauses (Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/915, Clause 7.7) leave it as "[SPECIFY TIME PERIOD]". So the client needs to know what its contract says, and to act before the period ends.

Why clients bring these emails to their accountant

  • The notices look like spam ("Updates to our subprocessor list") and are often ignored, so the objection window passes unnoticed.
  • AI tools change model hosts often, and a new host may sit outside the EU. That is a transfer under GDPR Chapter V (Articles 44 to 46).
  • Records and notices go out of date: the client's record of processing must list recipients and third-country transfers (Article 30(1)(d), (e)), and the privacy notice must name recipients or categories of recipients and transfers (Article 13(1)(e), (f)).
  • Customer and supplier questionnaires ask "list all sub-processors of your AI tools". The sheet becomes the answer.

Checklist: reading a sub-processor notice

  • Is the supplier a processor for the client? It processes personal data on the client's behalf (Article 28(1)). If the tool gets no personal data, there is no Article 28 step (row 6).
  • Find the authorisation clause in the DPA or terms: specific (client approves each one) or general (agreed list, client informed of changes). Article 28(3)(d) requires the contract to cover the conditions for engaging another processor. No clause at all: row 7, STOP and ask.
  • Note the notice period in the contract. The GDPR sets none; SCC Clause 7.7(a) Option 2 asks for notice "at least [SPECIFY TIME PERIOD] in advance" with "sufficient time to be able to object".
  • Where is the data processed? Outside the EU/EEA, Chapter V applies to onward transfers too (Article 44). Look for an adequacy decision (Article 45) or an Article 46 safeguard such as standard data protection clauses or binding corporate rules.
  • Same obligations passed down: the supplier must impose the same data protection obligations on the sub-processor and stays fully liable to the client for it (Article 28(4)). Under SCC Clause 7.7(c) the client can ask for a copy of the sub-processor agreement (it may be redacted).
  • Update the paperwork: record of processing (Article 30(1)(d), (e)), privacy notice (Article 13(1)(e), (f)), and keep the notice and any reply with the contract.

Email to the supplier: questions (template)

Subject: Your sub-processor notice of [date]: questions before [object-by date]

Hello,

We received your notice about [sub-processor name]. Under our data processing terms and GDPR Article 28(2) we may object to this change. Before [object-by date] please tell us: (1) what personal data of ours it will process and for what; (2) where it will process it, and if outside the EU/EEA, which safeguard under GDPR Article 45 or 46 applies; (3) that the sub-processor contract imposes the same data protection obligations as our agreement with you (Article 28(4)). Please treat this email as keeping our right to object open until you have answered.

Kind regards, [client name]

Subject: Objection to the addition of [sub-processor name]

Hello,

Referring to your notice of [date], we object under GDPR Article 28(2) and [clause number] of our data processing terms to the engagement of [sub-processor name] for our personal data, because [no transfer safeguard was named / the same data protection obligations are not confirmed / other reason]. Please confirm in writing that our personal data will not be processed by it, or tell us the options under our contract.

Kind regards, [client name]

Limits and open points

Guidance not read: we built this guide from the text of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR) and Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/915 on EUR-Lex. We did not read EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 on controller and processor, national authority guidance, or the list of Commission adequacy decisions for this guide; check them alongside this sheet. Open points: the GDPR does not say what happens if the client objects and the supplier goes ahead anyway, or how long "sufficient time" is; that depends on the contract. Treating a notice with no answer as accepted is our reading of a general authorisation, not a rule in the text. Many AI tools use their own standard terms rather than the Commission clauses; read the client's actual terms. The sheet sorts notices; it does not decide legal questions.

Frequently asked questions

Can a business object to a new sub-processor of its AI tool?

Yes, where the contract gives the supplier a general written authorisation: the supplier must inform the business of intended additions or replacements, giving it the opportunity to object (GDPR Article 28(2)). Under a specific authorisation, the supplier needs the business's prior written authorisation for each one.

How long is the notice period for sub-processor changes?

The GDPR does not set one. It is agreed in the contract; the Commission's standard contractual clauses (Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/915, Clause 7.7(a)) leave it as a period to be specified, with sufficient time to object before the sub-processor is engaged.

What if the new sub-processor is outside the EU?

The transfer must meet GDPR Chapter V, including onward transfers (Article 44): an adequacy decision (Article 45) or an appropriate safeguard such as standard data protection clauses or binding corporate rules (Article 46).

Who is responsible if the sub-processor fails?

The supplier must impose the same data protection obligations on the sub-processor and remains fully liable to the business for the sub-processor's performance (GDPR Article 28(4)).
