Does this AI tool need a DPIA? A one-page GDPR screening sheet for small EU businesses (the 9 criteria, a score per tool, 3 filled examples)

Updated 21 Oct 2026 · Disclosure: the EURO AI Register mentioned below is an Unadhi product with a free edition. This guide is for information only, not legal advice.

What you get: a finished DPIA screening sheet to fill once per AI tool that touches personal data: the three cases where the GDPR always requires a DPIA, the nine criteria the EU data protection authorities use (two or more usually means a DPIA is needed), a score and decision line, three filled example rows (made up, to show the level of detail), the four parts a DPIA must contain, and when you must consult your authority before you start.

The short answer

Under Article 35(1) GDPR, a controller must carry out a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) before processing that is likely to result in a high risk to people's rights and freedoms, "in particular using new technologies". One DPIA may cover a set of similar processing operations with similar high risks. If you have a data protection officer, you must ask for their advice (Article 35(2)).

To decide whether the risk is likely to be high, the Article 29 Working Party guidelines WP248 rev.01 (adopted 4 April 2017, last revised 4 October 2017) list nine criteria and say: "In most cases, a data controller can consider that a processing meeting two criteria would require a DPIA". Sometimes one criterion is enough. Many AI tools score two quickly, because new technology is itself a criterion.

Step 1: the three cases where a DPIA is always required

  1. Systematic and extensive evaluation of people based on automated processing, including profiling, on which decisions with legal or similarly significant effects are based (Article 35(3)(a)).
  2. Large-scale processing of special category data (Article 9(1)) or criminal-offence data (Article 10) (Article 35(3)(b)).
  3. Systematic monitoring of a publicly accessible area on a large scale (Article 35(3)(c)).

Any yes: you need a DPIA; skip to the decision line. Also check your own authority's published list of processing that always needs a DPIA (Article 35(4)); it may name AI uses such as employee monitoring or scoring.

Decision line: criteria met = [count]. 0: no DPIA, write one line saying why and date it. 1: no DPIA unless that one criterion is serious (write your reason). 2 or more: carry out a DPIA before you use the tool on real data. Re-run the screen when you change the tool, its settings, its data or its use (Article 35(11)).

Step 3: what the DPIA must contain (Article 35(7))

  • A systematic description of the processing and its purposes, including any legitimate interest you rely on. For an AI tool: what goes in (prompts, uploads, recordings), what comes out, who sees it, the supplier and its sub-processors. Your Article 30 entry for the tool is a good start.
  • Necessity and proportionality: why you need the tool for that purpose, and why less data or a less intrusive setting would not do.
  • The risks to people: for example wrong or biased outputs, people not knowing they are recorded, data reused to train the supplier's model, data kept too long, transfers outside the EU.
  • The measures that address each risk: settings, contracts, access limits, human review, notices to staff and customers, deletion periods.

Where appropriate, ask the people affected or their representatives for their views (Article 35(9)): with staff tools, that usually means the works council or staff representatives.

Step 4: do you need to consult your authority?

Only if the DPIA shows that the processing would result in a high risk without the measures you can take yourself, and you cannot bring it down (Article 36(1)). You then consult your data protection authority before you start. The authority gives written advice within up to eight weeks, which it can extend by six weeks for complex cases, telling you of the extension within one month (Article 36(2)). Send it the DPIA, the roles of each party, the purposes and means, your safeguards and your DPO's contact details if you have one (Article 36(3)).

How this links to the AI Act

If the AI tool is a high-risk AI system and you are the deployer, Article 26(9) of the AI Act tells you to use the information the provider must give you under Article 13 (the instructions for use) when you carry out your DPIA. Ask the provider for it in writing. Where you also have to carry out a fundamental rights impact assessment (Article 27), Article 27(4) lets you cross-refer to the relevant sections of your DPIA or include parts of it, so you do not write the same thing twice.
