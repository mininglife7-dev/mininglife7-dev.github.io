Shop camera check sheet for accountants: GDPR warning sign, 72-hour storage and AI camera features (EDPB Guidelines 3/2019, AI Act Articles 5 and 50(3)) for small business clients

What you get: a finished camera check sheet for accountants, tax advisers and bookkeepers to hand to small business clients with security cameras: shops, cafés, kiosks, small hotels and offices. One row per camera; you answer twelve short questions and the sheet gives a result (OK, FIX, CHECK BY HAND, STOP: REMOVE CAMERA or STOP: FACE RECOGNITION) plus a to-do list with the paragraph of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) guidelines behind each item. A copy-ready warning sign, a second-layer information sheet, a footage request log with the one-month deadline, a DPIA checkpoint and a client email follow. Made-up example rows show how it fills in.

Short answer: filming people with a camera is processing of personal data under the GDPR. For a private business the usual legal basis is legitimate interest (Article 6(1)(f)), which needs a real, present risk, a check that less intrusive means will not do, and a balancing of interests (EDPB Guidelines 3/2019, paragraphs 17-38). People must be told before they enter the filmed area: a warning sign (first layer) plus a full information sheet (second layer) with everything in GDPR Article 13 (paragraphs 110-118). Footage should in most cases be deleted after a few days; the longer beyond 72 hours, the more reasoning is needed (paragraph 121). Cameras with face recognition or other AI analytics bring in GDPR Article 9 and, for AI systems, the EU AI Act: emotion recognition at work is prohibited (Article 5(1)(f)), and people exposed to emotion recognition or biometric categorisation must be informed (Article 50(3)).

Why clients ask their accountant this question

  • The camera came with the shop fit-out or the alarm contract. Nobody wrote down why it is there, how long it records or who can watch it. The sheet turns that into one row per camera.
  • New cameras ship with "smart" features. People counting, age and gender estimates for advertising screens, "known visitor" face alerts. Some of these are AI systems under the EU AI Act and some trigger the GDPR rules for biometric data.
  • Staff ask. Employees in most cases do not expect to be monitored at work (paragraph 37), and national law or works agreements can set extra rules (paragraph 48, GDPR Article 88). Consent from employees is in most cases not a valid basis (paragraph 47).
  • A customer asks for the footage. The right of access applies, with a one-month deadline (GDPR Article 12(3)). The sheet has a log for that.

Step 2: tab 2, the warning sign (first layer)

Place it so people see it before they enter the filmed area, at about eye level. You do not have to show where the camera is, as long as it is clear which area is filmed (paragraph 113). The content below follows the EDPB's non-binding example (paragraphs 114-115). Fill in the brackets:

Video surveillance
Who is responsible: [business name, address] · Contact: [email, phone] · Data protection officer: [contact, or delete this line if none]
Why: [for example: to protect our premises and staff against theft and vandalism; our legitimate interest]
What matters most to you: [for example: footage is recorded and deleted automatically after [48 hours] unless an incident is under investigation; footage is shared only with the police or our insurer when needed / live view only, nothing is recorded]
Your rights: you can ask us for access to or erasure of your images, and you can object.
Full information: [at the till / on the poster by the door / at www.example.com/cameras / QR code] and by phone on [number].

Anything that could surprise people must be on the sign, for example transfers outside the EU or the storage period. If the sign says nothing about recording, people may assume live view only (paragraph 115).

Step 3: tab 3, the second-layer information sheet (GDPR Article 13)

Keep it at a central place (till, reception) and, ideally, at a web address or QR code that can be read without entering the filmed area (paragraph 117). It must contain everything Article 13 requires:

  • ☐ Name and contact details of the business, and of its EU representative where applicable (Art 13(1)(a)).
  • ☐ Data protection officer contact, where there is one (Art 13(1)(b)).
  • ☐ Purposes and legal basis (Art 13(1)(c)); for legitimate interest, the interest itself, for example protection of property (Art 13(1)(d)).
  • ☐ Recipients, for example the security company, insurer or police (Art 13(1)(e)).
  • ☐ Transfers outside the EU, for example a cloud recorder hosted abroad, and the safeguard used (Art 13(1)(f)).
  • ☐ Storage period, or the criteria for it (Art 13(2)(a)).
  • ☐ Rights: access, rectification, erasure, restriction, objection, portability (Art 13(2)(b)), and the right to complain to the data protection authority (Art 13(2)(d)).
  • ☐ If any camera relies on consent (for example a face recognition entrance that people opt into), the right to withdraw consent at any time (Art 13(2)(c)).

Checklist: data protection impact assessment and security

  • ☐ DPIA: required for systematic monitoring of a publicly accessible area on a large scale (GDPR Art 35(3)(c)) and for large-scale processing of special categories such as biometric data (Art 35(3)(b)). Each national authority publishes a list of processing that always needs a DPIA (Art 35(4)); check the client's country list. The EDPB expects that many video surveillance cases will need one (paragraph 137).
  • ☐ Write a short camera policy: who is responsible, purpose, where and when filming is allowed, storage, who may watch footage, how police or insurer requests are handled (paragraph 131).
  • ☐ Monitors placed so only authorised staff can see them; passwords and access rights for the recorder; changes to access logged (paragraph 135).
  • ☐ Switch off functions that are not needed, such as audio, zoom, unlimited pan and analytics (paragraph 129).
  • ☐ Handing footage to the police: legal obligation under national law (Art 6(1)(c)) when they ask in an investigation; when the business sends footage on its own initiative, check legitimate interest, usually met with a reasonable suspicion of a crime (paragraphs 57-59). Never post footage online for fun (paragraph 53).

When the camera has AI features (EU AI Act)

  • Emotion recognition (Article 3(39)): inferring emotions of people at the workplace is prohibited since 2 February 2025, except for medical or safety reasons (Article 5(1)(f); Article 113(a)). Where it is used on customers, the deployer must inform the people exposed (Article 50(3)).
  • Age, gender or similar categories from images: this can be a biometric categorisation system (Article 3(40)); deployers must inform the people exposed (Article 50(3)). Categorising people by biometric data to infer race, political opinions, trade union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs, sex life or sexual orientation is prohibited (Article 5(1)(g)). Under the GDPR, the EDPB says classification by age or gender without biometric templates for unique identification is not Article 9 processing (paragraph 80), but storing templates to recognise the same person coming back is (paragraph 82).
  • Face recognition of visitors: remote biometric identification systems are listed as high-risk in Annex III point 1(a), excluding pure one-to-one verification; the high-risk rules for Annex III systems apply from 2 December 2027 (Article 113 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744). Building or expanding facial recognition databases by untargeted scraping of CCTV footage is prohibited (Article 5(1)(e)).
  • People counting without identifying anyone is usually the lowest-risk feature; the GDPR rules in tab 1 still apply to the camera itself.
  • The Article 50 transparency duties apply from 2 August 2026, the general application date (Article 113).

Limits and open points

The EDPB guidelines (version 2.0, adopted 29 January 2020) do not go into national law, which differs between Member States (footnotes 7 and 18): several countries have their own camera rules, storage periods (paragraph 120), sign rules and workplace rules. They also do not cover processing by the police, which follows Directive (EU) 2016/680 (paragraph 61). Purely household cameras can fall outside the GDPR, but not once they film public space (section 2.3). Whether a given camera feature is an "AI system" under Article 3(1) of the AI Act depends on how it works; see our "is it an AI system?" check sheet. For staff monitoring, biometric features or large sites, get advice and check the national authority's guidance.

Frequently asked questions

How long may a shop keep camera footage?

As short as the purpose allows. The EDPB says damage is usually noticed within one or two days, footage should in most cases be deleted after a few days, ideally automatically, and the longer the period beyond 72 hours, the more justification is needed; for a small shop 24 hours is normally sufficient, with longer periods possible over closed weekends or holidays (paragraph 121). National law may set specific periods (paragraph 120).

Is a sign at the door enough?

The sign is the first layer. A second layer with all Article 13 information must also be available, for example at the till or via a web address (paragraphs 117-118). A sign does not make filming lawful on its own: it does not change what people can reasonably expect (paragraph 40).

Can staff consent to cameras at work?

In most cases no: because of the imbalance of power, employers should not rely on employee consent (paragraph 47). National law or works agreements may set specific rules (paragraph 48).
