Short answer. Three sets of EU AI Act rules already apply: banned AI practices and AI literacy measures (since 2 February 2025), rules for general-purpose AI models (since 2 August 2025) and transparency duties such as telling people they are talking to a chatbot (since 2 August 2026). The high-risk rules that many guides still date to August 2026 were postponed by the Digital Omnibus on AI to 2 December 2027 (hiring, education, credit scoring and other Annex III uses) and 2 August 2028 (AI in regulated products such as medical devices).

What changed in 2026 (the Digital Omnibus)

  • High-risk dates moved, mainly because the technical standards companies need were not ready.
  • AI literacy was reworded. Providers and deployers must still take measures to support staff AI literacy, but no fixed "sufficient level" is required. A simple record of what you did is sensible evidence; no formal credential is required.
  • A new ban on "nudification" apps and AI-generated child sexual abuse material, from 2 December 2026.
  • Machinery moved to Annex I Section B, and AI that only assists or optimises performance is not a "safety component" if its failure creates no health or safety risk. Fewer factory tools should end up high-risk.
  • SME relief (such as simplified technical documentation) now also covers small mid-cap companies.

Does it apply if you are outside the EU?

It can. The Act covers providers placing AI systems or general-purpose AI models on the EU market wherever they are based, and providers and deployers outside the EU whose AI output is used in the EU (Art. 2). A software company in India, the US or the UK selling an AI feature to EU customers should check. Purely internal use with nothing reaching the EU is usually outside it.

Provider or deployer: decide this first

Your duties depend on your role for each AI system. A provider develops an AI system and puts it on the market under its own name. A deployer uses one in its work, for example a recruitment tool bought as SaaS. For a bought-in high-risk tool, the risk-management system, technical documentation and conformity assessment are the vendor's job. The deployer's job (Art. 26, from 2 Dec 2027 for Annex III systems, 2 Aug 2028 for Annex I products) is to use it as instructed, assign trained human oversight, check input data, monitor it, keep logs for at least six months, and inform workers' representatives and affected people.

How to tell if a system might be high-risk

  1. Is it banned? For example emotion recognition of employees, social scoring, or untargeted scraping of face images. If yes, stop and get advice.
  2. Is it used for an Annex III purpose? Biometrics, critical-infrastructure safety, education (admissions, grading, proctoring), employment (recruiting, CV filtering, promotion, firing, monitoring), essential services (credit scoring, life and health insurance pricing, public benefits), law enforcement, migration, justice or elections.
  3. Does an exception apply? A narrow procedural or preparatory task may fall outside high-risk (Art. 6(3)), but a system that profiles people is always high-risk.
  4. Is it a safety component of a regulated product such as a medical device? Then the 2028 date applies.

Most AI in a typical small business (writing assistants, spam filters, forecasting, quality inspection) is minimal risk. The AI Act then mainly asks for AI literacy and, where relevant, transparency. Over-classifying wastes money; under-classifying an HR or credit tool creates real risk.

The 10-point readiness checklist

  • Named one person who owns AI governance
  • Listed every AI system and AI feature you build, buy or let staff use
  • Recorded your role (provider or deployer) for each one
  • Screened each against the banned practices, and stopped anything flagged
  • Checked each for Annex III uses and product safety components, and written down why
  • Sent anything "possibly high-risk" or "unclear" for legal review
  • Made sure chatbots say they are AI, and AI-generated media is labelled where required
  • Adopted an AI acceptable-use policy and briefed staff (AI literacy), with a simple log
  • Asked vendors of HR, credit or other sensitive AI tools for their AI Act plans
  • Put the December 2027 and August 2028 dates in your plan, with owners

Common mistakes

  • Using a 2024 or early-2026 template that still says high-risk rules start in August 2026.
  • Giving a deployer the provider's checklist (Annex IV documentation, conformity assessment) for a tool they bought.
  • Ignoring what already applies: banned practices, AI literacy and transparency.
  • Claiming an organisation-wide AI Act approval. The Act has no general sign-off for organisations; conformity assessment applies only to specific high-risk systems.

Frequently asked questions

Do EU AI Act high-risk rules apply from August 2026?

No. The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) moved them to 2 December 2027 for Annex III uses such as recruitment and credit scoring, and to 2 August 2028 for AI safety components of regulated products.

What parts of the EU AI Act apply already?

Banned practices and AI literacy measures since 2 February 2025, general-purpose AI model rules since 2 August 2025, and transparency duties and full enforcement (including Commission fines on general-purpose AI model providers) since 2 August 2026; the penalty rules themselves have applied since 2 August 2025.

Does the EU AI Act apply to companies outside the EU?

It can. It covers providers placing AI on the EU market wherever they are based, and providers and deployers outside the EU whose AI output is used in the EU.

Is AI literacy training mandatory?

Article 4 requires providers and deployers to take measures to support the AI literacy of staff who operate or use AI on their behalf. Since the 2026 amendment no specific level is mandated, and no formal credential is required.
