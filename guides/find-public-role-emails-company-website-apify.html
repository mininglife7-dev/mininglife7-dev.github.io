Find public role emails (sales@, press@, support@) on a company website without guessing

Updated 14 Oct 2026 · Disclosure: the Contact Details Scraper linked below is an Unadhi product on the Apify Store. This guide is free, the manual method needs no tool at all, and the Apify method works with any contact scraper.

Short answer: do not guess sales@company.com. Companies publish the shared inbox they want you to use, usually in the footer, the contact, about or press page, the impressum or legal notice, the privacy policy, or /.well-known/security.txt. Check those pages, copy the address exactly as shown, and note the page you found it on. For tens or hundreds of sites, paste the homepages into a contact scraper on Apify with personal emails switched off and robots.txt respected, then export the role inboxes to CSV. Use them for one-to-one messages that fit the inbox, not bulk cold email.

Why guessing is a bad idea

  • It bounces. Many companies have no sales@, or route it to a ticket system with a different address. Bounced mail hurts your own sender reputation.
  • It lands in the wrong place. A press question sent to support@ gets a ticket number, not a reply.
  • It is unnecessary. If a company wants to hear from buyers, journalists or partners, it says where. Your job is to find that, not to invent it.

Step 1: decide which inbox you actually need

Your reason for writingInbox names companies often useWhere it is usually published
Buy, wholesale, quotesales@, orders@, b2b@, wholesale@Contact page, footer, "For business" page
Story, interview, media kitpress@, media@, pr@Press or newsroom page, about page
Partnership, reseller, integrationpartners@, partnerships@, bd@Partners page, about page
Problem with an order or productsupport@, help@, care@Help centre, contact page, order emails
Data protection requestprivacy@, dpo@, dataprotection@Privacy policy
Security vulnerabilitysecurity@/.well-known/security.txt, security page
General enquiryinfo@, hello@, contact@Footer, contact page, impressum

These are common naming habits, not rules. A company may use none of them. Only use an address you have actually seen published.

Step 2: check the pages where companies publish inboxes

  • Footer of the homepage: many small companies list one general inbox there.
  • Contact page (/contact, /contact-us, /kontakt): often split by department.
  • About and press pages: press and partnership inboxes usually live here.
  • Impressum or legal notice (/impressum, /legal, /mentions-legales): companies in Germany and Austria, for example, are generally required to publish an electronic contact address here.
  • Privacy policy: under GDPR the controller's contact details must be given, so a privacy or data protection inbox often appears here.
  • /.well-known/security.txt: a small text file (standard RFC 9116) with a Contact: line for security reports. Use it only for security issues.
  • Careers page: a jobs@ or careers@ inbox, only for applications.

Two quick tricks: press Ctrl+F and search for @ on each page, and hover over "Email us" buttons to see the mailto: address behind them. Some sites show a form only; in that case the form is the published channel, so use it.

The "found on" column matters: it is your proof that the company published the address, and it lets you re-check later if the page changes. If the only address you can find is a person's name (for example firstname.lastname@), leave it out of this sheet. That is personal data and needs a separate, lawful reason.

Step 5: check the result and use it one to one

  • Export the dataset as CSV and open it next to your sheet from Step 3. Keep the page URL for every address.
  • Drop anything that failed the MX check, and anything that looks like a person rather than a team.
  • Pick one inbox per company that matches your reason for writing (Step 1). Do not send the same message to info@, sales@ and support@ at once.
  • Write a short, specific, one-to-one message. A role inbox is not permission to add a company to a newsletter or a bulk sequence.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the privacy@ or security@ inbox as a sales channel. Those exist for data requests and vulnerability reports only.
  • Scraping marketplaces or social networks for seller contacts. Their terms usually forbid it; the Actor above skips them by design.
  • Keeping an old list for months. Inboxes change; re-check the "found on" page before you write.
