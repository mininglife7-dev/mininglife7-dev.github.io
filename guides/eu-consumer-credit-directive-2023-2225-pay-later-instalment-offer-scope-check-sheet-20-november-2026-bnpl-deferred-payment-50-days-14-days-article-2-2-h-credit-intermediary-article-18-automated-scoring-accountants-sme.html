Short answer: the new Consumer Credit Directive, Directive (EU) 2023/2225, applies from 20 November 2026, 18 days from today (Article 48(1)). It repeals the 2008 directive (Article 47). Two changes matter most for small businesses. First, free "pay later" from the seller itself stays outside only if no third party offers the credit, no interest or other charges are added (only limited late-payment charges) and the consumer pays in full within 50 days of delivery; for large online sellers that are not SMEs the limit is 14 days (Article 2(2)(h)). Second, buy-now-pay-later through a third party is in scope (recitals 16 and 17), and a shop that presents it for payment can be a credit intermediary (Article 3(12)). Where scoring is automated, consumers get a right to human intervention, an explanation and a review (Article 18(8)). Agreements already signed before 20 November 2026 stay under the old rules, except some articles for open-end agreements (Article 2(2)(l)).

Why this lands on the accountant's desk

  • Payment terms are set up in the books. The adviser sees the 30-day consumer invoices, the 60-day terms with an admin fee, the finance partner's commission statements and the instalment plans first.
  • A small fee changes the answer. A EUR 5 "invoice fee" or "admin fee" on a consumer deferral takes it out of the Article 2(2)(h) exclusion, because the price must be paid without any other charges.
  • Many Member States were late. The national laws are what businesses apply, and some may still be changing (see the limits section). The sheet tells you which offers to check against the national text first.

Step 3: the 10-question checklist

  1. Have we listed every way the client lets consumers pay later: invoice terms, instalments, BNPL buttons, finance partners, store cards?
  2. Are the customers consumers, people acting outside their trade (Art 3(1))? B2B terms are outside this Directive.
  3. Who gives the credit: the client itself, or a bank, finance company or BNPL provider?
  4. Does the consumer pay any interest, admin fee or invoice fee for paying later? Limited late-payment charges under national law do not count (Art 2(2)(h)(ii)).
  5. Is the full price paid within 50 days of delivery, or within 14 days if the client is a large online seller that is not an SME (Art 2(2)(h))?
  6. If a third party gives the credit, does the client present or offer it, do paperwork, or conclude it, and is the client paid for that (Art 3(12))?
  7. Do ads for the credit carry "Caution! Borrowing money costs money" or equivalent wording, and are they fair, clear and not misleading (Art 7, 8(1))?
  8. Are any boxes for credit or extras such as insurance pre-ticked (Art 15)?
  9. If the client is the creditor, is there a written creditworthiness procedure using income and expenses, without special-category data or social networks (Art 18(1), (3), (4))?
  10. Is scoring automated or AI-based? Then plan the human-intervention, explanation and review route (Art 18(8)-(9)), the personalised-offer notice (Art 13), and the AI Act high-risk duties from 2 Dec 2027 (Annex III point 5(b); Reg (EU) 2026/1744).

Frequently asked questions

When do the new EU consumer credit rules apply?

Directive (EU) 2023/2225 on credit agreements for consumers repeals Directive 2008/48/EC with effect from 20 November 2026. Member States had to adopt their national laws by 20 November 2025 and must apply them from 20 November 2026 (Article 48(1), Article 47). Agreements already existing on 20 November 2026 stay outside the new Directive, except that some articles apply to existing open-end agreements (Article 2(2)(l)).

Our client lets customers pay 30 days after delivery, free. Is that now consumer credit?

Not on that alone. Article 2(2)(h) excludes deferred payments where the supplier itself, with no third party offering credit, gives the consumer time to pay for its own goods or services, free of interest and other charges (only limited late-payment charges under national law), with payment in full within 50 days of delivery. For large online sellers that are not SMEs the limit is 14 days and no third party may offer or purchase the credit.

Is buy now, pay later through a third-party provider covered?

Yes, the exclusion in Article 2(2)(h) only applies where no third party offers credit, and recital 17 names 'buy now, pay later' schemes as the example of a third party offering credit. The third-party provider is the creditor. A shop that presents or offers that credit, or does paperwork for it, in return for remuneration, can be a credit intermediary under Article 3(12); merely introducing a consumer to a creditor is not intermediation under that definition.

What changes when a credit decision uses automated scoring or AI?

Article 18(8) gives the consumer the right to human intervention: a clear explanation of the creditworthiness assessment including the logic and risks of the automated processing, the chance to express their view, and a review of the assessment and decision. Article 18(9) requires informing a rejected consumer without delay and, where applicable, that the assessment was automated and how to contest it. Article 13 requires telling consumers when an offer is personalised by automated processing of personal data. Separately, the AI Act lists AI systems used to evaluate creditworthiness of natural persons as high-risk (Annex III point 5(b)).
