Short answer: the new EU Product Liability Directive, Directive (EU) 2024/2853, "shall apply to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026" (Article 2(1)). Under it, "product" includes "software" (Article 4(1)), "irrespective of whether the software is stored on a device, accessed through a communication network or cloud technologies, or supplied through a software-as-a-service model", and AI system providers "should be treated as a manufacturer" (Recital 13). Liability is no-fault, cannot be "limited or excluded by a contractual provision" towards the injured person (Article 15), and a manufacturer that can still push updates is not let off for defects caused by software updates or "a lack of software updates or upgrades necessary to maintain safety" (Article 11(2)). Member States must transpose it "by 9 December 2026" (Article 22(1)); the old Directive 85/374/EEC keeps applying to products placed on the market before that date (Article 21).

Why accountants and their clients should look at this now

Many small clients now ship software: an app sold with a gadget, an AI feature inside a booking or health tool, firmware for a device, a white-label tool sold under their own brand. Products first placed on the EU market or put into service from 10 December 2026 fall under the new rules. Three points matter for advice this quarter:

  • Who pays. The developer, anyone who puts "their name, trademark or other distinguishing features" on the product, and anyone who "develops, manufactures or produces a product for their own use" is a manufacturer (Article 4(10)). If the manufacturer is outside the EU, the importer, the authorised representative and, failing both, the fulfilment service provider are liable (Article 8(1)(c)). A distributor becomes liable if it cannot name an EU operator or its own supplier "within one month" of a request (Article 8(3)). Anyone who substantially modifies a product outside the manufacturer's control becomes its manufacturer (Article 8(2)).
  • What damage counts. Death or personal injury, including "medically recognised damage to psychological health"; damage to property, except the product itself and "property used exclusively for professional purposes"; and "destruction or corruption of data that are not used for professional purposes" (Article 6(1)). So a business-to-business tool can still be exposed where a person is injured or private data or property is damaged.
  • How long. Claims must be brought within three years of awareness (Article 16(1)) and expire 10 years after the product was placed on the market, or after a substantial modification (Article 17(1)); 25 years for latent personal injury (Article 17(2)). That is the time frame for the evidence file and the provision or insurance discussion.

What the Directive does not cover: "information" such as "the content of digital files, such as media files or e-books or the mere source code of software" (Recital 13); "free and open-source software that is developed or supplied outside the course of a commercial activity" (Article 2(2)); and data leaks under GDPR, which stay under GDPR (Article 2(4)(a); Recital 20). It also does not oblige anyone to provide updates (Recital 51); it makes the lack of safety updates count when the manufacturer still controls the product.

Step 3: tab 3, two templates

(a) Reseller reply when asked to name the manufacturer. A distributor that cannot identify an EU-established manufacturer, importer or representative, or its own supplier, within one month of the request becomes liable (Article 8(3)). Put the request date in a cell and use =EDATE(B1,1) for the reply deadline (our working rule for "one month").

Subject: Your request to identify the economic operator for [product, model, version]

Thank you for your request received on [date]. Under Article 8(3) of Directive (EU) 2024/2853 as implemented in [country], we identify the following for [product, model, version, serial or licence number]: manufacturer [legal name, address]; [importer / authorised representative] in the EU [legal name, address]; and the supplier that supplied it to us [legal name, address]. Please write to them directly. We keep a copy of this reply.

(b) Starting clause for small software component suppliers. A manufacturer that builds software into its product has no right of recourse against a micro or small software-component manufacturer (as defined in Commission Recommendation 2003/361/EC) where it "contractually agreed ... to waive that right" (Article 12(2)). Liability to the injured person itself cannot be waived (Article 15). Starting wording for a lawyer to adapt (our working draft):

"The Customer integrates the Software as a component in its own products. The Customer waives any right of recourse against the Supplier, as provided for in Article 12(2) of Directive (EU) 2024/2853 and the national law implementing it, in respect of damage caused by a defect in the Software. The Supplier confirms that it is a [micro / small] enterprise within the meaning of Commission Recommendation 2003/361/EC on the date of this agreement."

What to tell a client in one paragraph

"Software and AI you sell or put your name on and first place on the EU market from 10 December 2026 count as products under the new EU product liability rules. If they injure someone, damage private property or wipe private data, you can be liable without fault, your terms cannot exclude that, and if you can still push updates you stay responsible for safety updates. Keep a dated evidence file for each release for at least 10 years, check your insurance covers software and data loss, and if you resell products from outside the EU, make sure you can name an EU importer or representative within a month of being asked." Stay inside your professional rules and refer liability questions to a lawyer. Member States can also keep or add national rules removing the "state of the art" defence for some products (Article 18), and existing national schemes must be notified by 9 December 2026 (Article 18(1)), so check the client's country.
