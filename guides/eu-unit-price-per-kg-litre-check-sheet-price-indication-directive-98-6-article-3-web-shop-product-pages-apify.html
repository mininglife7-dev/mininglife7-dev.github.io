Short answer: under Article 3(1) of the Price Indication Directive (98/6/EC), a trader offering products to consumers must indicate both the selling price and the unit price. The unit price is the final price, including VAT and all other taxes, for one kilogram, one litre, one metre, one square metre or one cubic metre, or another single unit customarily used in that Member State for that product (Article 2(b)). It is not needed where it is identical to the selling price (Article 3(1)); for products sold in bulk only the unit price is needed (Article 3(3)); and any advertisement that mentions the selling price must also show the unit price (Article 3(4)). Both prices must be "unambiguous, easily identifiable and clearly legible" (Article 4(1)). Member States can waive or narrow the rule in several ways (Articles 3(2), 5, 6 and 10), so check the national law.

Step 3: tab 2, offer and advert check (one row per Black Friday email, post or banner)

Article 3(4) covers advertisements, not only product pages: any advertisement that mentions the selling price of a product must also indicate the unit price. Article 6a adds that a price reduction announcement must show the prior price (the lowest price in at least the 30 days before the reduction). The Commission's 2021 guidance on Article 6a says that where a reduction is announced by a general statement (for example "20% off everything"), the prior price does not have to be on the same medium; it must then be shown at the point of sale, on the price tags or in the price section of the online shop.

Frequently asked questions

Does the unit price rule apply to online shops?

Article 1 covers products "offered by traders to consumers" and does not limit this to physical shops, and Article 3(4) extends it to advertisements that mention a selling price. A shop selling only to businesses is outside the Directive, because "consumer" means a natural person buying outside their trade or profession (Article 2(e)).

Can a small shop skip the unit price?

Only if national law says so. Article 6 let Member States exempt certain small retail businesses for a transitional period where unit pricing would be an excessive burden; Article 5 lets them waive the unit price for products where it would not be useful or would confuse, and draw up a list of non-food products where it still applies; Article 3(2) lets them exclude products supplied as part of a service, auctions, and works of art and antiques; and Article 10 allows stricter national rules. These choices differ between countries.

Per kg, or per 100 g?

Article 2(b) names one kilogram, litre, metre, square metre or cubic metre, or "a different single unit of quantity which is widely and customarily used in the Member State concerned" for that product. Which other units are allowed is a national matter; check your country's price indication law before changing the per-kg column. Recital 8 of the Directive gives the reason: some products are customarily sold in quantities other than one kilogram or litre.

Is the deposit on bottles part of the price?

No, according to the Court of Justice in famila-Handelsmarkt Kiel (C-543/21, 29 June 2023): the selling price includes unavoidable and foreseeable components the consumer must pay (para. 19), but a deposit is refunded when the container comes back, so it is not part of the "final" selling price under Article 2(a) (paras. 20 to 23, 29). Showing it separately lets consumers compare prices (para. 27).

What happens if the unit price is missing?

Member States set the penalties, which must be "effective, proportionate and dissuasive" (Article 8(1), as amended by Directive (EU) 2019/2161).
