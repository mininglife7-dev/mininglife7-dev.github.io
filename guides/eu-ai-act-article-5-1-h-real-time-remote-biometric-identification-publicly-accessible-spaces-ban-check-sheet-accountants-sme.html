Short answer: from 2 February 2025 (AI Act Article 113), the Union prohibits the use of ‘real-time’ remote biometric identification systems in publicly accessible spaces for the purposes of law enforcement, unless and in so far as such use is strictly necessary for one of the objectives in Article 5(1)(h)(i)/(ii)/(iii), and then only under the conditions in Article 5(2)–(7) (authorisation, safeguards, FRIA, registration). Point (h) is without prejudice to GDPR Article 9 for biometric processing for purposes other than law enforcement — so a shop’s commercial live face-match usually sits outside this ban on the law-enforcement test, but still needs a GDPR Article 9 (and often Article 5(1)(e)/(g) / Annex III) check. Definitions: remote biometric identification system (Article 3(41)), real-time (Article 3(42)), post-remote (Article 3(43)), publicly accessible space (Article 3(44)), law enforcement (Article 3(46)). The Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) added intimate-image and CSAM bans as Article 5(1)(ba)/(bb) from 2 December 2026; it did not amend Article 5(1)(h) (our Omnibus check). Fines for Article 5 breaches sit under Article 99(3): up to EUR 35 000 000 or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher (for SMEs, the lower of those two caps — Article 99(6)).

Why this lands on the accountant's, tax adviser's or agency's desk

  • Shop CCTV + live face matching is the client question after a vendor pitch or a complaint. Article 5(1)(h) itself is a law-enforcement rule; tell the client the commercial row is usually out of this ban but not out of GDPR Article 9.
  • Venue entry and security contractors advising SMEs often blur "security" with law-enforcement support — mark LE purpose Unsure and keep the evidence.
  • The rule has already applied since 2 February 2025. Anything still live that fits the LE real-time public-space test without a verified exception is overdue, not a future deadline.
  • Article 5 fines are the top tier (Article 99(3)). One row per practice answers with the article behind it.

Step 3: checklist

  1. Is an AI system involved (Articles 3(1), 5(1)(h))?
  2. Is it placed, put into service or used in the EU, or is its output used in the EU (Article 2)?
  3. Is it a remote biometric identification system (Article 3(41))?
  4. Is it real-time under Article 3(42) (not merely post-remote under Article 3(43))?
  5. Is the place a publicly accessible space (Article 3(44) — shops and venues can qualify; prisons/border control and online spaces do not)?
  6. Is the use for law enforcement (Article 3(46))? If not, Article 5(1)(h) is out of scope — but GDPR Article 9 still applies.
  7. If LE: is an Article 5(1)(h)(i)/(ii)/(iii) exception claimed, and do Articles 5(2)–(7) authorisation, FRIA and registration conditions hold?
  8. If the row is STOP or IN SCOPE, has the practice been switched off? The ban has applied since 2 February 2025 (Article 113).
  9. Have we told the client that Article 5 fines are the top tier (Article 99(3)/(6))?
  10. Who decided, on what date, and when is the next review?

Frequently asked questions

What does Article 5(1)(h) of the AI Act ban?

It prohibits the use of ‘real-time’ remote biometric identification systems in publicly accessible spaces for the purposes of law enforcement, unless and in so far as such use is strictly necessary for one of the objectives in Article 5(1)(h)(i), (ii) or (iii) (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). Point (h) is without prejudice to GDPR Article 9 for processing of biometric data for purposes other than law enforcement. Definitions: remote biometric identification (Art 3(41)), real-time (Art 3(42)), publicly accessible space (Art 3(44)), law enforcement (Art 3(46)).

When did the real-time remote biometric identification rule start to apply?

Chapters I and II of the AI Act, which include Article 5, apply from 2 February 2025 (Article 113). Non-compliance with Article 5 can attract administrative fines of up to EUR 35 000 000 or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher; for SMEs the lower of those two caps applies (Article 99(3) and 99(6)).

Does a shop’s live face-match CCTV trigger the Article 5(1)(h) ban?

Usually not under Article 5(1)(h) itself, if the use is commercial loss-prevention and not for law enforcement. Article 5(1)(h) is limited to law-enforcement use in publicly accessible spaces. Point (h) expressly leaves GDPR Article 9 biometric rules in place for non-law-enforcement processing. Tell the client to run separate checks for GDPR Article 9, Article 5(1)(e)/(g) where relevant, and Annex III remote biometric identification.

What are the Article 5(1)(h) exceptions?

Use may be allowed only where strictly necessary for: (i) targeted search for specific victims of abduction, trafficking or sexual exploitation, and search for missing persons; (ii) prevention of a specific, substantial and imminent threat to life or physical safety, or a genuine and present or foreseeable threat of a terrorist attack; or (iii) localisation or identification of a person suspected of an Annex II offence punishable by at least four years. Even then, Articles 5(2)–(7) require prior authorisation, safeguards, FRIA and registration rules.

Did the Digital Omnibus change Article 5(1)(h)?

No. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 amended Article 5 by inserting points (ba) and (bb) on non-consensual intimate material and child sexual abuse material (from 2 December 2026). We searched the Omnibus Article 5 amendment text: it does not amend Article 5(1)(h) real-time remote biometric identification.
