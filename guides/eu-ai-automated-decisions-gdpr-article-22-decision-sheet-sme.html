The short answer

The GDPR gives people the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, that produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects them (Article 22(1)). Recital 71 gives two everyday examples: automatic refusal of an online credit application, and e-recruiting without any human intervention. Such a decision is only allowed if it is necessary for a contract with the person, authorised by EU or national law with safeguards, or based on explicit consent (Article 22(2)). In the contract and consent cases you must at least offer human intervention, a way for the person to express their view and a way to contest the decision (Article 22(3)), and you must tell people about it up front (Articles 13(2)(f) and 14(2)(g)).

Two Court of Justice rulings matter for small businesses. In SCHUFA (C-634/21, 7 December 2023) the Court held that an agency's automated credit score is itself an automated decision where the business that receives it draws strongly on it to establish, implement or terminate a contract. In Dun & Bradstreet Austria (C-203/22, 27 February 2025) it held that a person asking about an automated decision is owed an explanation of the procedure and principles actually applied to their data, concise and intelligible, and that a complex formula or a description of every technical step is not enough.

The EU AI Act adds a second layer only for some uses: hiring and worker management, creditworthiness of individuals and life or health insurance pricing are high-risk under Annex III. After the Digital Omnibus on AI, the high-risk duties for these uses apply from 2 December 2027 (Article 113(c)(i)). The GDPR rules above apply now.

Two-question test: is this an Article 22 decision?

  1. Is it solely automated? Yes, unless a person with the authority and skill to change the outcome actually reviews each case before it takes effect. Rubber-stamping, or a person who always follows the score, counts as automated. A bought-in score you rely on strongly counts too (SCHUFA).
  2. Does it have a legal or similarly significant effect? Yes for refusing credit or pay-later, rejecting a job applicant, ending a contract, cutting someone off from a service they depend on, or decisions about housing, work or education. Usually not for an ordinary product recommendation; write down your reasoning for anything in between.

Two yes answers: fill all 17 lines below. One or no yes: still fill lines 1 to 5 and 13, so you can show why Article 22 does not apply, and keep the normal GDPR duties (legal basis, notice, access requests) in place.

Paste-ready lines

Notice line (application form, checkout or job ad)

We use automated checks, including [a credit score from [agency] / an AI screening tool from [supplier]], to decide [whether we can offer purchase on invoice / which applications go to interview]. The main factors are [for example: payment history with us, the address check, the score from [agency]]. If the result is a no, you can ask a member of our team to look at your case again, tell us your side and challenge the decision by writing to [contact] within [30] days. More information: [link to privacy notice].

Reply when someone asks "why did your system say no?"

Thank you for your message. Your [application / order on invoice] was assessed automatically using [tool]. The decision was mainly based on: [factor 1, and how it counted], [factor 2], [factor 3]. The data we used were [list] from [source]. [Name/role] has now reviewed your case [and confirmed / changed] the result because [reason]. If you disagree, you can reply with any information you want us to consider, or complain to [your data protection authority].

The reply follows what the Court described in C-203/22: the procedure and principles actually applied to this person's data, in plain words. Check the official source and adapt to your case; national law may add rules.

Explanation pack template (half a page per decision)

  • Purpose: what the decision is for, in one sentence
  • Data used: each input and where it comes from (form, your records, agency, device data)
  • Main factors: the 3 to 5 inputs that move the result most, and in which direction
  • Thresholds: in words, for example "a score below the supplier's 'high risk' band leads to a refusal of invoice payment; other payment methods stay available"
  • What the person can do: correct wrong data, give context, choose another option
  • Human review: who, how fast, how to ask
  • Not used: list inputs you deliberately excluded, such as health data or nationality
