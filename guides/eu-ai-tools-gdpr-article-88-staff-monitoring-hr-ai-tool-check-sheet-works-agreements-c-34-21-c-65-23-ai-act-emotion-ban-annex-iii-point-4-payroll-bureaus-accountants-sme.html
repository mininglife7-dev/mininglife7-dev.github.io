Short answer: GDPR Article 88 lets EU countries, by law or collective agreements (including works agreements, recital 155), set more specific rules for staff data. The Court of Justice has made two points clear. First, such national rules count only if they include the suitable and specific safeguards in Article 88(2), otherwise they must be disregarded (C-34/21, 30 March 2023). Second, a works agreement does not lower the GDPR floor: it must still meet Articles 5, 6(1) and 9, and a court can fully review whether the processing was really "necessary" (C-65/23, 19 December 2024, a case about real staff data copied to a US group parent's HR cloud during a test). Separately, the AI Act has banned AI that infers staff emotions since 2 February 2025 (except for medical or safety reasons), and lists recruitment, task allocation and performance monitoring tools as high-risk, with deployer duties for those from 2 December 2027.

Why this lands on the accountant's desk

  • Payroll bureaus and bookkeepers hold the staff data and are often asked by clients which HR or monitoring tool is "OK to switch on".
  • Clients buy tools with AI features switched on by default: mood scores in call-centre software, productivity scores in time trackers, AI rota planners. The AI Act ban and the GDPR questions arrive with the next update, not with a new purchase.
  • Supplier questionnaires, bank and insurer due diligence increasingly ask whether staff are monitored, on what legal basis and whether a DPIA exists.

Step 3: the 10-question checklist

  1. Have we listed every tool that records, scores or watches staff, including AI features inside existing software?
  2. Does any tool infer emotions, mood or stress from faces, voice or typing? If yes and not for a medical or safety reason, switch the feature off (AI Act Art 5(1)(f)).
  3. Is each tool used to evaluate staff or to support decisions on hiring, pay, promotion, dismissal or tasks? If yes, treat it as likely high-risk under Annex III point 4 and plan for 2 Dec 2027.
  4. Have we written down the legal basis for each tool (GDPR Art 6(1))?
  5. Where we rely on a works or collective agreement, does that agreement itself respect Articles 5, 6(1) and 9 and include the Article 88(2) safeguards (C-34/21, C-65/23)?
  6. Could a less intrusive way do the same job, for example dummy data for a test, aggregate reports instead of individual scores (Art 5(1)(c))?
  7. Are health, union, biometric or other Article 9(1) data involved, and if so, which Article 9(2) condition applies?
  8. Have staff been told in writing, naming the tool, the purpose, the recipients and how long data is kept (Art 13)?
  9. Have we done a DPIA where the monitoring or evaluation is likely to result in a high risk (Art 35)?
  10. Does any data leave the EU/EEA, including to a group parent, and if so on what Chapter V basis?

Frequently asked questions

Can a works agreement or collective agreement make staff monitoring lawful on its own?

Not on its own. The Court of Justice held that national rules adopted under GDPR Article 88(1), including rules in a collective agreement, must still require compliance with Article 5, Article 6(1) and Article 9(1) and (2), and that the parties' margin of discretion on what is 'necessary' does not stop a national court from carrying out a full review (C-65/23, 19 December 2024, rulings 1 and 2).

Is an AI tool that reads staff emotions allowed at work?

Since 2 February 2025 the AI Act prohibits the placing on the market, putting into service for this purpose or use of AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where the system is intended for medical or safety reasons (Article 5(1)(f), applying under Article 113(a)).

Are HR and staff-monitoring AI tools high-risk under the AI Act?

Annex III point 4 lists AI systems intended for recruitment or selection and AI systems intended to make decisions affecting terms of work-related relationships, promotion or termination, to allocate tasks based on individual behaviour or personal traits, or to monitor and evaluate performance and behaviour. Under Article 113 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the high-risk rules for Annex III systems apply from 2 December 2027. Article 6(3) exceptions can apply and are not assessed by this sheet.

Can we rely on staff consent?

GDPR Article 4(11) requires consent to be freely given, and recital 43 says consent should not be a valid ground where there is a clear imbalance between the person and the controller. Recital 155 lets national law or collective agreements set the conditions for employee consent. Our reading: for staff monitoring, plan for another legal basis and check national law.
