Short answer: NIS2 (Directive (EU) 2022/2555) applies from 18 October 2024 (Article 41(1)) mainly to medium-sized and larger entities in the sectors of its Annexes I and II (Article 2(1)). Most small clients are not covered themselves, but their covered customers must manage "supply chain security, including security-related aspects concerning the relationships between each entity and its direct suppliers or service providers" (Article 21(2)(d)), taking into account "the overall quality of products and cybersecurity practices of their suppliers and service providers, including their secure development procedures" (Article 21(3)). That is why the questionnaire arrives. Answer it truthfully, with evidence, and never tick a box the client cannot back up.

Why accountants and bookkeepers should look at this now

  • The questionnaire lands on small firms. Recital 85 says covered entities "should in particular be encouraged to incorporate cybersecurity risk-management measures into contractual arrangements with their direct suppliers and service providers". A web agency, IT support firm, software maker, payroll bureau or cleaning company with system access can all receive one.
  • Some customers have a binding list. For cloud, data centre, managed service and managed security providers, online marketplaces, search engines, social networks, DNS, TLD and trust service providers, Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 sets out what the supply chain policy and contracts must contain (Annex, point 5). Other covered entities often reuse that list, so it is the best guide to what you will be asked.
  • AI tools count as suppliers. A chatbot, meeting note-taker or AI writing tool that touches customer systems or data is an ICT service from a "direct supplier or service provider". Customers increasingly ask which AI tools see their data. Your client should know the answer before the questionnaire arrives.
  • A wrong "yes" is worse than an honest "not yet". Contract answers can become warranties. The sheet's result column will not produce READY unless there is an evidence document dated within the last year.

Article 2(2) brings in some entities "regardless of their size", such as providers of public electronic communications networks or services, trust service providers, top-level domain name registries and DNS service providers, the sole provider in a Member State of an essential service, and certain public administration entities. NIS2 also says Article 3(4) of the Recommendation's Annex "shall not apply" (Article 2(1)). Each Member State transposed NIS2 into its own law, so check the client's national act and registration rules before relying on B6.

For MFA, the Implementing Regulation asks the entities it covers to authenticate users "by multiple authentication factors or continuous authentication mechanisms … where appropriate, in accordance with the classification of the asset to be accessed" (Annex 11.7.1), and to use "strong identification, authentication such as multi-factor authentication" for privileged and system administration accounts (Annex 11.3.2(a)). Expect customers to ask about admin accounts first.

Step 3: tab 3, contract clause check (the eight supplier clauses)

Implementing Regulation 2024/2690 says the entities it covers must make sure their supplier contracts specify, "where appropriate through service level agreements" and "where appropriate", the following (Annex 5.1.4). Put one row per clause in the customer's draft contract: B in the contract (Yes/No), C can we meet it today (Yes/Partly/No), D note.

Result for each row: =IF(B2="No","NOT ASKED",IF(C2="Yes","OK TO SIGN",IF(C2="Partly","NEGOTIATE OR ADD PLAN DATE","DO NOT SIGN AS WRITTEN"))) (our working rule). For clause (d), the customer itself reports significant incidents with an early warning "within 24 hours" and a notification "within 72 hours" of becoming aware (NIS2 Article 23(4)(a) and (b)), so it will want to hear from the supplier fast. Agreeing an internal target that the client can meet is our working rule, not a legal deadline for the supplier.

Step 4: tab 4, the client's own supplier list (including AI tools)

The Implementing Regulation asks the entities it covers to keep "a registry of their direct suppliers and service providers", including "contact points for each direct supplier and service provider" and "a list of ICT products, ICT services, and ICT processes provided" (Annex 5.2). The same list answers topic (d) for a small supplier. Columns: A supplier, B contact point (security or support email), C ICT product or service, D is it an AI tool (Yes/No), E customer data it sees (none, contact data, files, systems access), F MFA enforced (Yes/No), G admin accounts, H last reviewed. Cover reply template

Subject: Supplier security questionnaire – [our company]

Thank you for the questionnaire dated [date]. We have answered each question against what we do today and attached the evidence named in each answer. Where we answer "Partly" or "No", we give the date by which we plan to close the gap. AI tools that may process your data are listed in the attached supplier list with their contact points. We will tell [contact] without undue delay if we have an incident that may affect your systems or data. If a question does not fit a business of our size, we have explained what we do instead.

The last sentence reflects the proportionality idea in the Implementing Regulation: where entities "cannot implement some of the technical and the methodological requirements … due to their size", they "should be able to take other compensating measures that are suitable to achieve the purpose of those requirements" (Recital 5). That recital speaks about covered entities, but it is a fair way to frame a small supplier's answers.

What to tell a client in one paragraph

"You are probably not under NIS2 yourself, but your larger customers are, and the law makes them check their suppliers. When a security questionnaire arrives, answer every question truthfully, attach evidence that is less than a year old, give dates for gaps, and never claim a certification or a control you do not have. Turn on multi-factor authentication on email, accounting and AI tool accounts first, keep a list of your own suppliers and AI tools, and read the incident-notice, audit and termination clauses before you sign." Check the client's national law and any sector rules first; refer contentious contract terms to a lawyer.
